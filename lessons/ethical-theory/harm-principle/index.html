Before the First Session

Five gut-checks, with no right answers. Say where you stand. The council will come back to them once the new constitution is ratified.

The government may forbid adults from doing things that harm only themselves. Being deeply offended by something is a kind of harm. A doctor should be able to override an adult patient's foolish-but-informed choice. Vaccine mandates are a violation of personal liberty. Some things should be illegal simply because they are immoral.

The Wizard's Handiwork

Ozma reviews the realm's legal code, which lurches between two opposite regulatory failures:

  • The law is smotheringly paternalistic, locking green spectacles onto citizens for their own comfort and banning travel for their own safety.
  • Yet it abandons public safety by leaving sleep-inducing poppy fields unfenced and letting fraudulent market vendors deceive buyers.
  • The Scarecrow spots the core defect: the state protects citizens from themselves while abandoning them to each other.

The Council Consults a Book

Glinda produces a slim volume from the Great Book of Records' library: On Liberty (1859), by John Stuart Mill.

  • Mill was not only worried about tyrant kings, though Oz has had those. He was worried about the tyranny of the majority.
  • A whole society can press everyone into one mold, by law and by social pressure.
  • His question is exactly the council's question: when may the community rightfully use power over one of its members?
  • His answer, he warns, is "one very simple principle."

One Very Simple Principle

"The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

  • Mill adds that a person's own physical or moral good is never a sufficient warrant for state compulsion.
  • Friends and communities may reason, plead, or warn, but society cannot use legal force against an individual for their own sake.
  • Over himself, over his own body and mind, the mature individual is sovereign.
  • The principle applies strictly to people in the maturity of their faculties, excluding children and those unable to reason for themselves.

Why Value Liberty?

Mill does not appeal to abstract natural rights. As a utilitarian, he argues liberty must earn its keep by advancing human well-being:

  • Human authorities are fallible, and overriding individuals wagers that the state can never be mistaken.
  • Societies discover which ways of life truly succeed only by permitting open experiments in living.
  • Choosing for oneself is essential to flourishing, whereas a person steered like a puppet cannot develop genuine individuality.
  • This utilitarian case anchors political liberalism: the state protects personal liberty while remaining neutral on how to live.

The Council's Minutes

Power may be used over a citizen against their will only to prevent harm to others; their own good is not a sufficient warrant. Compelling people for their own good is called paternalism.

The Line Everything Depends On

The whole principle turns on one core distinction, though in practice few actions touch only one person.

Affects primarily you: your body, your personal risks, your diet, and your plans. Society may advise and warn, but cannot compel.

An adult who eats an unhealthy diet, gambles personal wages, or climbs treacherous mountain peaks alone.

Causes or directly risks harm to others: their bodies, their property, or their rights. Here legal coercion is legitimate.

A manufacturer who dumps industrial waste into a drinking river, or a vendor who knowingly sells poisoned food.

Mill concedes this boundary is disputed: actions that seem self-regarding often impose indirect burdens on families and public services.

The Intervention Test

Mill's decision procedure: when may the community rightfully compel an individual?

The Poppy Question

The sleep-inducing poppy fields serve as a parable for dangerous recreation and recreational drugs:

  • Banning entry to protect adults from their own foolishness is hard paternalism, overriding voluntary self-regarding choice.
  • Uninformed travelers who wander in unaware are a different case, because genuine choice requires understanding the hazard.
  • Mill supports warning signs, poison labels, and child safety rules, which inform agency without banning it.
  • The council drafts rules that mark the hazards and register guides, while letting informed adults enter at their own risk.

Decision Point: The Poppy Fields

Not graded. The council is split three ways and so is the room.

Ban entry. Some choices harm the chooser badly enough that the state may step in. Signpost the fields, register the guides, ban bringing children, and let adults walk in. Nothing. A free adult needs no signposts from a council.

Mill's Bridge

A traveler is about to step onto a damaged bridge with no time to warn him. May you seize him?

  • Mill says yes: stopping him briefly honors his presumed desire not to fall into the chasm.
  • Once warned, an informed adult who still insists on crossing cannot rightfully be restrained.
  • This illustrates soft paternalism: pausing someone only to verify their choice is informed.
  • Overriding an informed, voluntary decision is hard paternalism, which Mill rules out.

The Pamphleteer of Emerald Square

A pamphleteer publishes an article claiming that local grain merchants are starving the poor. The merchants demand a ban.

  • Mill famously argued that opinions claiming corn dealers starve the poor must circulate freely in the press.
  • However, shouting those same words to an excited, torch-bearing mob outside a dealer's home may justly be punished.
  • Printed pamphlets invite reflection and debate, whereas incitement before an agitated crowd creates an immediate hazard.
  • The boundary turns on the real-world circumstances of the speech rather than government hostility to the message.

"What If the Pamphlet Is False?"

The merchants argue the claims are false. Why protect falsehood? Mill offers three utilitarian arguments:

  • If a suppressed opinion turns out to be true, society is robbed of truth because human censors are never infallible.
  • If the dissenting view contains only partial truth, it provides the missing piece needed to correct prevailing opinions.
  • Even when entirely false, opposing views prevent received truths from decaying into unexamined, dead dogma.
  • Public argument forces citizens to understand the grounds of their convictions rather than reciting them by rote.

The Problem of Deep Offense

What about conduct that causes no physical injury or financial loss, but generates intense moral outrage? Consider a radical group picketing private family funerals with offensive slogans, like the real-world Snyder v. Phelps dispute.

Feinberg's Offense Principle

A century after Mill, philosopher Joel Feinberg argued the Harm Principle needs a carefully restricted supplement:

  • Subjective offense, outrage, and disgust differ from harm because they do not damage a person's vital interests or rights.
  • However, profound and unavoidable public offense (such as picketing a private funeral) may warrant narrow legal limits.
  • Feinberg's balancing test evaluates how intense the offense is, whether it was avoidable, and its social value.

Hedging the Offense Principle

Liberal legal systems adopt narrow compromises when regulating public offense:

  • Regulations impose reasonable time, place, and manner buffer zones around funerals rather than censoring specific viewpoints.
  • The serious danger is that offense is deeply subjective, allowing majorities to label any controversial idea offensive.
  • If hurt feelings count as harm, the Harm Principle collapses and paternalistic censorship returns under a new name.
  • This is why Feinberg restricted legal intervention to severe, unavoidable public nuisances.

Mapping State Authority

Liberal jurisprudence matches different state powers to three distinct tiers of conduct.

The Health Chapter

The council examines medical law, where the old constitution's contradictions were most glaring:

  • The old code forced citizens who refused a doctor's remedy into restraint for their own recovery, practicing pure paternalism.
  • Yet during infectious disease outbreaks, it provided zero quarantine powers or reporting systems to protect the public.
  • The old law repeated its signature errors: coercing the competent individual while abandoning public health.

The Right to Refuse Treatment

Consider a competent, informed elderly patient who declines high-risk surgery, preferring comfort care at home:

  • Forcing unwanted medical treatment on a competent patient under physical restraint is textbook hard paternalism.
  • Mill's verdict is unyielding: over one's own body, the individual is sovereign, so physicians may advise but cannot compel.
  • Modern bioethics and constitutional law reach this same conclusion through informed consent and the right to bodily integrity.
  • Mill's anti-paternalist framework is a foundational source for modern patient autonomy.

Strike or Keep?

Epidemics and Harm to Others

The council considers public health measures during an outbreak of an airborne, contagious disease:

  • Refusing an infectious disease vaccine or quarantine is fundamentally different from refusing personal surgery.
  • An infected person transmits pathogens to vulnerable infants, the elderly, and immunocompromised neighbors.
  • Because transmission is other-regarding conduct, preventing harm to others is the primary justification for public health mandates.
  • Compulsory vaccination (as in Jacobson v. Massachusetts) protects innocent third parties rather than practicing paternalism.

The Safety Harness Debate

Debating mandatory harnesses in fast wagons mirrors modern legal battles over seatbelt and motorcycle helmet laws.

An unharnessed driver risks primarily his own life. Compelling safety equipment forces competent adults to obey rules for their own good.

"Post crash fatality statistics at every station, but leave the final choice to the adult driver."

Drivers rarely crash alone: emergency crews face hazards, public funds pay for emergency trauma care, and families suffer devastation.

"Serious injuries impose heavy financial and emotional burdens on the broader community."

This tension is central to modern law: stretching harm to include diffuse public costs risks allowing the state to regulate almost any risky lifestyle habit.

Decision Point: The Safety Harness

Not graded. Where you stop this argument is roughly what decides which kind of liberal you are.

No. Post the injury figures at every wagon-yard and let competent adults decide. Yes. Rescuers take risks, the public purse pays, a family loses its provider. Only where the cost to others is large and easy to point at.

When Is a Choice Truly Voluntary?

The council investigates reports that commercial poppy-field operators deliberately engineer maze-like paths and hidden exits:

  • Mill's principle protects voluntary and informed choices, but manipulative design actively subverts decision-making capacity.
  • Real-world analogs include casino architecture, loot boxes, and algorithmic infinite scroll designed to defeat self-control.
  • Restricting exploitative design targets the bad actor's deception rather than penalizing the individual user's choices.

Harms With No Single Victim

A core challenge that 19th-century liberalism struggled to address at scale is aggregate or diffuse environmental harm:

  • A single factory chimney or car exhaust pipe causes no measurable injury on its own.
  • However, thousands of emissions combine to produce toxic smog that damages an entire population's lungs.
  • The Harm Principle easily justifies environmental regulation once the law accounts for cumulative contributions to collective harm.

When Falsehood Causes Rapid Harm

Another major contemporary challenge is viral medical misinformation, such as false claims that a vaccine is lethal:

  • Mill assumed public deliberation provides ample time for truth to refute and overtake dangerous falsehoods.
  • When panic spreads instantly during a lethal outbreak, third parties suffer preventable death before rebuttal can take effect.
  • These modern problems illustrate where the Harm Principle transitions from a neat formula into a complex constitutional balancing test.

Critics of Liberal Neutrality

The council hears formal dissents from traditions that argue the state should promote goodness, not merely prevent harm:

  • Perfectionism, originating with Aristotle, holds that political institutions should actively cultivate moral excellence and virtue.
  • Legal moralism asserts that a society requires a shared moral code to survive, giving the law authority to punish harmless immorality.
  • Lord Devlin famously defended this view against H.L.A. Hart in 1960s Britain, arguing private vice undermines social order.

The Communitarian Challenge

A deeper critique questions whether completely isolated, sovereign individuals actually exist:

  • Communitarian philosophers argue that human identities, duties, and purposes are inextricably shaped by community ties.
  • If our lives are deeply interdependent, almost no significant action is purely self-regarding or costless to others.
  • Liberals reply that while human interdependence is real, using it to justify state coercion opens the door to authoritarianism.
  • The Harm Principle therefore endures as a vital legal presumption that places the burden of proof on state intervention.

The Hardest Vote

The Drafting Committee's Glossary

Overriding an informed adult's choice for their own good is hard paternalism; stepping in only to ensure a choice is informed and voluntary is soft paternalism. Banning conduct because it is immoral, though it harms no one, is legal moralism. In the liberal tradition, liberty is the default, and the burden of proof falls on whoever would compel.

The Council Adjourns

The old constitution is retired to the archives, replaced by a legal framework grounded in Mill's liberalism.

  • The Harm Principle permits state coercion solely to prevent non-consensual harm to others, never for a citizen's own physical or moral good.
  • Mill grounds liberty on utilitarian foundations: fallibility, experiments in living, and the cultivation of human individuality.
  • The framework distinguishes self-regarding conduct from other-regarding harm, and soft paternalism from hard paternalism.

Principles for Modern Governance

  • Freedom of speech protects even false or unpopular opinions, penalizing speech only when circumstances create direct incitement to harm.
  • Medical ethics respects competent treatment refusal as self-regarding, while justifying quarantine and vaccine rules to halt contagion.
  • Ongoing legal debates center on diffuse collective harms, behavioral manipulation, and competing communitarian or perfectionist ideals.
