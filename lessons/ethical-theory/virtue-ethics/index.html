The Philosopher

"He was my father's student," Athena says. "Well, my father's student's student. Plato taught him. But he thought Plato was too abstract."

Athena tells you about Aristotle. She says he was her father's student's student. Plato taught him, but he thought Plato was too abstract.
  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE) studied at Plato's Academy for twenty years, then broke away.
  • He tutored Alexander the Great, founded the Lyceum, and studied everything: biology, politics, logic, poetry.
  • Where Plato looked up to ideal Forms, Aristotle looked around, at how people live, thrive and fail.
  • His ethics starts from a simple question: "What does a flourishing human life actually look like?"

Eudaimonia

"Every mortal wants to be happy," Athena says. "But most of you have no idea what that actually means."

Now, eudaimonia. Athena says every mortal wants to be happy, but most of you have no idea what that actually means.
  • Everything we do aims at some good. What is the final good, wanted for its own sake?
  • Aristotle calls it eudaimonia (εὐδαιμονία). It does not mean "happiness" but flourishing: living well across a whole life.
  • It is an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue (NE I.7). It is a way of living, not a feeling.
  • Not pleasure alone, not wealth (ask Midas), not fame. Each can be part of a good life; none is the whole.

The Golden Mean

"Now pay attention," Athena says. "This is where most of you go wrong."

The golden mean. Athena says to pay attention, because this is where most of you go wrong.
  • A virtue is a stable disposition (hexis), not a one-off act. The brave person doesn't just act bravely once; they are brave, reliably.
  • Each virtue sits between two vices: excess and deficiency. Courage lies between rashness and cowardice; generosity between prodigality and stinginess.
  • The mean is not an arithmetic midpoint. It shifts with the person and the situation.
  • Some feelings and actions have no mean at all. There is no right amount of spite or theft.
  • What counts as courage for a trained warrior differs from what counts for a civilian. Seeing which response the moment calls for takes phronesis, or practical wisdom.

The Rage of Achilles

"Look at Achilles," Athena says. "Unmatched physical courage, and a total disaster of a human."

The rage of Achilles. Look at Achilles, Athena says. Unmatched physical courage, and a total disaster of a human.
  • He was the Greeks' best fighter in their long war against Troy.
  • His own commander humiliated him over a share of the spoils, so he sat in his tent while his allies were slaughtered.
  • He returned only when the Trojans killed his closest friend, then killed their prince and dragged the body behind his chariot.
  • "My father's poets called it 'heroic,'" Athena snorts. "I call it a lack of self-government."

Courage: Finding the Mean

Aristotle uses courage as the classic virtue. But it's not just "not being afraid."

Courage: finding the mean. Aristotle uses courage as the classic virtue. But it's not just not being afraid.
  • Deficiency (Cowardice): Fleeing in fear, prioritizing safety over honor.
  • Excess (Rashness): Rushing headlong into danger without reason. Achilles in battle.
  • The Mean (Courage): Facing the right things, for the right reason, in the right way, at the right time.
  • His rage, sulking and then desecrating a fallen foe, is a second failing. Aristotle calls it irascibility: the excess of good temper, not of courage.

The Flight of Icarus

Athena sweeps her hand, and the scene changes to a boy soaring over the Aegean. "Next, a boy who thought he was a bird."

The flight of Icarus. Athena sweeps her hand, and the scene changes to a boy soaring over the Aegean. Next, she says, a boy who thought he was a bird.
  • Trapped in the Labyrinth, the master inventor Daedalus built two sets of wings made of feathers and wax for himself and his son, Icarus.
  • Before they flew, Daedalus gave a strict warning: Do not fly too high, or the sun will melt the wax. Do not fly too low, or the damp sea will weigh down the feathers.
  • But once in the air, the thrill of flight overcame Icarus. He flew higher and higher, the wax melted, and he plunged into the sea.

The Literal Middle Path

"Daedalus wasn't just giving flight instructions," Athena notes. "He was explaining the Mean."

The literal middle path. Athena notes that Daedalus wasn't just giving flight instructions. He was explaining the Mean.
  • Icarus's tragedy is the Doctrine of the Mean made literal.
  • The Deficiency (Damp Sea/Timidity): Flying too low, being too fearful to rise and escape.
  • The Excess (Blazing Sun/Rashness): Flying too high, intoxicated by pride and thrill.
  • The Mean (The Middle Way): temperance and moderation, escaping safely by recognizing your limits.

Practice Makes Virtuous

"How do you think my heroes got good at fighting?" Athena asks. "Practice. Ethics is no different."

Practice makes virtuous. How do you think my heroes got good at fighting? Athena asks. Practice. Ethics is no different.
  • Aristotle calls this habituation (ethismos): "We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts" (NE II.1).
  • Virtue is not a set of rules you memorize. It's a stable disposition (hexis) built over time.
  • Just as you learn to play the lyre by playing the lyre, you learn to be patient by practicing patience.
  • But practice alone isn't enough. You need to know *what* to do in each unique situation.

The Cunning of Odysseus

Athena points to her favorite mortal. "Odysseus isn't the strongest. He's the one who gets home."

The cunning of Odysseus. Athena points to her favorite mortal. Odysseus isn't the strongest, she says. He's the one who gets home.
  • Trapped by the Cyclops, he did not charge in blind fury, which would have meant death. He got the giant drunk, said his name was "Nobody," and blinded him.
  • To hear the Sirens without wrecking, he had his crew plug their ears with wax and lash him to the mast.
  • Back in Ithaca he passed as a beggar for days, enduring insults to survey the household before striking.

Phronesis: Wisdom in Action

Odysseus shows phronesis, or practical wisdom. It is the master virtue that coordinates all the others.

Phronesis: wisdom in action. Odysseus shows phronesis, or practical wisdom. It is the master virtue that coordinates all the others.
  • Phronesis is the ability to perceive what a situation requires and find the right mean.
  • In the cave, raw courage without phronesis would have been suicide (rashness). Odysseus's restraint was the true mean.
  • "Phronesis is situational intelligence," Athena explains. "It connects intellectual smarts with a good character. Without it, cleverness is just a weapon."

The Tragedy of Medea

Athena's voice drops. "And then we have Medea. Brilliant, powerful, and utterly consumed."

The tragedy of Medea. Athena's voice drops. And then we have Medea, she says. Brilliant, powerful, and utterly consumed.
  • Medea used her magic to help Jason steal the Golden Fleece, betraying her own family for him. But years later, Jason abandoned her to marry the princess of Corinth.
  • Blinded by jealousy and betrayal, Medea plotted a horrific revenge. She sent a poisoned robe to Jason's new bride, who died in agony.
  • To inflict the maximum possible pain on Jason, she chose to murder their own children, escaping in a chariot sent by the sun god Helios as Jason wept.

The Nightmare of Akrasia

"She wasn't stupid," Athena says. "She knew exactly what she was doing. That's what makes it terrifying."

The nightmare of akrasia. She wasn't stupid, Athena says. She knew exactly what she was doing. That's what makes it terrifying.
  • Euripides gives Medea a famous line: "I know what evil I am about to do, but my passion is stronger than my reason."
  • The line is Euripides's, not Aristotle's. But it describes exactly what he calls akrasia: acting against your own better judgment.
  • Unlike a truly vicious person who thinks doing bad is good, the akratic person *knows* what is right but is overcome by desire or anger.
  • Without self-mastery and phronesis, Medea's brilliant cleverness became a tool of pure destruction.

The Theft of Fire

"You can't flourish alone," Athena says. "Even the Titan who made you learned that the hard way."

The theft of fire. You can't flourish alone, Athena says. Even the Titan who made you learned that the hard way.
  • Prometheus pitied humanity, cold and helpless in the dark. Defying Zeus, he stole fire in a hollow fennel stalk and gave it to mortals.
  • With fire came civilization, science and the arts. Zeus chained him to a rock in the Caucasus, where an eagle ate his liver daily.
  • "A noble act," Athena muses. "But a life of eternal torture on a rock is not what we call a happy ending."

The Political Animal

Aristotle famously wrote: "Man is by nature a political animal" (Politics I.2).

The political animal. Aristotle famously wrote that man is by nature a political animal. That's from the Politics, book one.
  • For Aristotle, humans cannot fully flourish in isolation. We need a polis (community/city-state) to practice virtue and live well.
  • Prometheus gave humanity the tools to build a community, but he was cast out of it. An individual on a rock, even a noble one, cannot achieve eudaimonia.
  • Flourishing requires shared laws, education, and social institutions. Virtue is co-authored with the community you inhabit.

The Loom of Penelope

"If you want to see virtue that sustains an entire kingdom," Athena says, "look at Penelope."

The loom of Penelope. If you want to see virtue that sustains an entire kingdom, Athena says, look at Penelope.
  • With Odysseus missing twenty years, 108 suitors overran Ithaca, demanding her hand and eating the palace bare.
  • Her trick: she would marry once a burial shroud was finished. She wove it by day and unravelled it by torchlight at night.
  • Three years of that, protecting her son, her household and the throne by patient intelligence.

Philia: Virtue in Community

Penelope's story shows that virtue is relational. It requires philia (friendship/broad social loyalty).

Philia: virtue in community. Penelope's story shows that virtue is relational. It requires philia, which means friendship or broad social loyalty.
  • Aristotle claims friendship is one of the greatest external goods for eudaimonia (NE VIII).
  • The highest form is virtue-friendship, where friends love each other for their character, helping each other grow.
  • Friendships of usefulness and of pleasure are real friendships too, only lesser ones.
  • Penelope's virtue wasn't a solo act of heroic pride like Achilles's; it was a communal anchor that kept the social fabric of Ithaca from tearing apart.

The Tradition Lives

"Mortals spent centuries arguing about rules and calculations," Athena says. "Then some of them remembered to ask the question that matters."

The tradition lives. Mortals spent centuries arguing about rules and calculations, Athena says. Then some of them remembered to ask the question that matters.
  • After centuries dominated by utilitarianism ("maximize happiness") and deontology ("follow the rules"), virtue ethics came roaring back in the 20th century.
  • Why? Because rules and calculations feel incomplete without asking: what kind of person should I be?
  • Three modern philosophers picked up Aristotle's project and ran with it, each in a different direction.

Three Modern Paths

Each updates Aristotle for the modern world: same root, different branches.

Three modern paths. Each updates Aristotle for the modern world: same root, different branches.
  • MacIntyre (After Virtue, 1981): virtues live inside practices and traditions.
  • Shared activities with internal goods; ongoing communal narratives.
  • His charge: modern individualism has fragmented our moral vocabulary, and we need to recover it.

Three Modern Paths: Foot

The second path is Foot.
  • Foot (Natural Goodness, 2001) called this neo-naturalism: virtues are natural excellences of a species.
  • We already judge living things this way. Deep roots are an excellence in an oak; a lioness who will not feed her cubs has gone wrong as a lioness.
  • Justice is ours. A human being who lacks it is defective as a human, in the same plain sense.

Three Modern Paths: Hursthouse

The third path is Hursthouse.
  • Hursthouse (On Virtue Ethics, 1999): an action is right if and only if it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do.
  • The standard complaint about virtue ethics is that it tells you what to be and not what to do.
  • This answers it. Faced with a decision, ask what a courageous or generous or just person would do here. That is a question you can actually work on.
