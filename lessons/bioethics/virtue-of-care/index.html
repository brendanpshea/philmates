"I Am Not a Warm Person"

"Ask anyone in town," Dr. March says, setting down her bag. "I don't do cheerful bedside small talk."

  • She is blunt, direct, and visibly impatient with administrative pretense.
  • Yet when a neighbor receives a terrifying diagnosis, Jo is the first clinician they call.
  • How can someone who lacks an easy bedside manner still excel at patient care?
  • Because care is an acquired clinical virtue, not an outgoing temperament.

Before Rounds

Five initial value questions, with no right or wrong answers. State where you stand now, and we will revisit them at the end of the day.

Caring is a personality trait. Some people have it and some don't. A clinician who follows every guideline correctly has done the job, whether or not the patient felt cared for. It is possible to care too much, and that is a real professional failing, not just a strength taken too far. Care is mostly about feelings. Competence is a separate matter. Whether clinicians can care for patients well depends more on how their workplace is run than on their own character.

"I Was Processed"

Every clinical box was checked. Nobody looked him in the eye.

  • Mr. Laurence, 82, was discharged after three days of inpatient care for heart failure.
  • Medications were reconciled, instructions printed, follow-up booked, and forms signed in eleven minutes.
  • By every hospital quality metric and chart audit, the discharge was completely flawless.
  • Yet he left clutching a five-page packet he could not read, telling his family: "I was processed."

Why Rule-Following Falls Short

Checklists ensure procedural compliance. They cannot guarantee ethical care.

  • Autonomy became paperwork: getting a signature on a form rather than ensuring genuine understanding.
  • Beneficence became medication titration without asking what he feared about going home alone.
  • Principles tell clinicians what duties are owed, but rules cannot tell you how to listen, perceive, or respond.
  • The virtue of care is not an optional extra added to principles. Cultivating care is what makes it possible to reliably fulfill them.

Two Moral Questions

Ethics asks two complementary questions: principles address action; virtues address character.

Public, verifiable action-guides. Clinicians specify them, weigh them in dilemmas, and justify them systematically.

Mr. Laurence's discharge satisfied every formal principle requirement on paper.

Stable traits of character. They govern how a clinician perceives human needs and determine the manner in which care is delivered.

Clinical ethics holds that principles and virtues are partners rather than rivals.

Diagnosing the Gap

Respect for autonomy, because valid informed consent was never obtained. The clinical character and attentiveness that allow principles to be genuinely fulfilled. Beneficence, because the medical discharge plan was clinically mistaken. Justice, because other ward patients were unfairly prioritized ahead of him.

Virtue as a Disposition

"I do not feel warm," Jo says. "I do care. Those are different things, and the distinction matters."

  • A virtue is a stable, acquired disposition of character.
  • It involves internal motivation: you act because you value the good, not just from compliance.
  • Aristotle called it a settled state (hexis), not a passing mood. You can care well even on a hard day.
  • It is not a fleeting feeling, not an innate personality type, and not a rulebook.

How Virtue Is Cultivated

"Nobody is born with clinical character," Jo says. "You learn it in stages."

  • First, from parents: Watching Marmee care for sick neighbors taught her attentiveness early.
  • Next, from mentors: Senior clinicians taught her to look past the chart to see the patient.
  • Finally, through habit: Deliberately sitting at every bedside until it became second nature.
  • Virtue is not an innate gift; it is built through patient, guided repetition.

Cultivating Clinical Character

Virtues are not mastered by memorizing codes of ethics. They are developed through guided practice and mentorship.

  • Medical training teaches clinical procedures, diagnostic pathways, and electronic charting systems.
  • Learning how to perceive unspoken distress requires observing mentors and receiving constructive feedback.
  • Dr. March's role with resident Amy is helping a capable, protocol-driven clinician look beyond the screen.
  • Mentoring feedback must itself model care: attentive, constructive, and supportive rather than punitive.

Care and Flourishing

Why cultivate a virtue at all? The ancient answer concerns the clinician as much as the patient.

  • Aristotle's term eudaimonia means human flourishing: living well over a whole life.
  • Virtues are not an exhausting tax on life; they are part of what makes a life good.
  • Practicing genuine care brings purpose to the caregiver, not just benefit to the patient.
  • When care becomes so ungrounded that it destroys the carer, something has gone wrong.

Evaluating the Concept

A virtue is a stable disposition of character, not a solitary good act. Virtues are acquired through repeated practice, much like clinical skills. A moral virtue involves internal motivation to do what is right, not just rule compliance. A virtue is an overwhelming emotional feeling of personal sympathy. You are either born with a caring virtue or you can never acquire it. A virtue is a rigid algorithmic checklist that specifies exact actions in advance.

Care as Skilled Labor

Hannah is a home health aide arriving at her fourth client's home at dusk in the snow.

  • Hospital posters treat "care" as a slogan. In clinical ethics, it is demanding, skilled work.
  • Care requires emotional commitment combined with the active willingness to act.
  • It is everything clinicians do to sustain, assist, and repair vulnerable human lives.
  • Notice what this demands: not performative cheerfulness, but dependable presence and skilled effort.

The Four Phases of Care

Beth, 24, has lived with chronic illness for years. Caring for her requires four distinct phases.

  • 1. Attentiveness: Noticing the need. Beth has grown quiet and stopped asking about medications.
  • 2. Responsibility: Stepping in to address her withdrawal rather than passing it to night shift.
  • 3. Competence: Executing care skillfully. Kind words with incorrect medication is failure, not care.
  • 4. Responsiveness: Checking how the care landed with Beth—because only the recipient knows.

The Instruments of Care

Biomedical ethics identifies five focal virtues that serve as practical instruments of care.

  • Compassion provides awareness of suffering. Discernment judges what this patient needs right now.
  • Trustworthiness ensures reliability. Integrity means practicing well even when unobserved.
  • Conscientiousness is the follow-through: orders placed, labs verified, families updated.
  • The nursing code of ethics opens with compassion and respect for the dignity of every patient.

Care vs. Warmth

Warmth is an innate personality temperament; care is a cultivated clinical virtue.

A natural personality trait that varies by individual. It can be easily simulated for a short time, and its absence does not make a clinician incompetent.

A cheerful clinician who neglects a promised lab check was pleasant, not caring.

A learned, disciplined orientation evident when patients are frustrated and clinic hours are stretched thin. It is inseparable from technical competence.

A tired, direct clinician pulling up a chair at the end of a long day to explain a difficult diagnosis.

Defining the Elements

A virtue is a stable disposition of character. Care involves emotional commitment to a person and a willingness to act on their behalf. Care without competence is sentimentality.

Recognizing Complete Care

An acquaintance who expresses deep sorrow about her illness but takes no constructive action. A resident who reviews her lab orders accurately but never speaks directly with her. A nurse who notices Beth has gone quiet, inquires gently, adjusts the plan, and follows up later. A relative who insists on an unproven intervention that Beth has already explicitly declined.

Detachment and Over-Involvement

Clinical care can fail in two opposite directions. Both harm the patient, and one also depletes the caregiver.

The patient is treated primarily as a case or a dataset. Protocols are completed, but personal presence is entirely absent.

Amy's hurried eleven-minute discharge where the patient felt processed.

A lack of boundaries where the caregiver takes on every burden personally, leading to exhaustion, loss of objectivity, and burnout.

Laurie working consecutive extra shifts while sick and answering family messages at midnight.

Paternalism as Misdirected Care

Meg is the eldest sister, teaching part-time, raising two children, and serving as Aunt March's primary unpaid caregiver.

  • Convinced that Aunt March could no longer live safely on her own, Meg quietly scheduled an assisted-living tour without consulting her.
  • Meg acted out of deep love and anxiety, yet her intervention crossed into unjustified paternalism.
  • Care that ceases to listen to the patient ceases to respect their agency. It begins doing things to a person rather than with them.
  • In clinical ethics, this is the intersection where beneficence and respect for autonomy must be balanced with discernment.

The Dilemma of the Soup

Jo lives next door to Mr. Laurence. On a freezing Sunday, she has a pot of soup and knows he is home alone.

  • Medical ethics codes strictly warn against dual relationships: becoming a neighbor or friend can cloud clinical judgment.
  • If she brings the soup, does she compromise her objective medical authority as his physician?
  • Yet in a small town where patients are also neighbors, refusing a simple gesture of kindness feels inhuman.
  • The soup is steaming on the stove. Two genuine ethical intuitions pull in opposite directions.

Decision Point: Sunday Afternoon

Not graded. Experienced clinicians often evaluate this dilemma differently.

Bring the soup. Compassionate care does not end at the office door. Do not bring it. Dual relationships inevitably threaten objective medical judgment. Bring it as a neighbor, and state clearly that she is visiting as a friend.

When Care Becomes Martyrdom

Laurie has covered ten extra ICU shifts this month. Today he arrived with a fever.

  • He is beloved by patients and always volunteers when the hospital is understaffed.
  • He believes that sacrificing personal sleep and health is the ultimate proof of clinical dedication.
  • Yet an exhausted, feverish nurse cannot maintain vigilance or protect patients from error.
  • Does genuine clinical care have limits, or is endless self-sacrifice its highest ideal?

Evaluating Over-Involvement

It demonstrates the highest ideal of clinical virtue because he sacrificed himself for the unit. It violates an administrative hospital policy on shift limits, but carries no broader ethical meaning. Because clinical care has no appropriate boundaries, his dedication cannot be ethically questioned. It represents an unsustainable excess that undermines his ability to care safely for patients and himself.

Autonomy and Non-Abandonment

Aunt March is 88, has capacity, and has snipped off her hospital wristband. She refuses admission.

  • The principle of autonomy is clear: a competent adult has the right to refuse treatment.
  • Care adds what the principle leaves out: honoring a refusal is not the same as walking away.
  • Jo respects the choice while staying involved: planning home symptom control and checking back Thursday.
  • Her choice sits inside a web: Meg, Hannah, Jo. Care attends to the relationships sustaining her.

Specifying Benefit and Harm

Beth apologizes whenever staff enter. Principles tell us to benefit and not harm; care defines what those mean for Beth.

  • Beneficence needs context: Beth values attending her niece's recital over two extra days of observation.
  • Attentive care discovers that priority through dialogue, earning the trust required for her to speak.
  • Nonmaleficence has blind spots: no checklist names the harm of a patient afraid of being a burden.
  • Attentiveness catches subtle distress, ensuring principles serve the person rather than the chart.

Justice and the Care Economy

The Hummels are a farmworker family with a sick infant and no insurance. Hannah, their aide, is paid hourly by an agency.

  • Meg's unpaid hours, Hannah's modest wages, and the Hummels' pharmacy receipt expose real inequalities.
  • Care work is historically underpaid. A virtue of care at the bedside without structural justice is incomplete.
  • Care ethics also addresses partiality: clinicians naturally feel dedicated to their assigned patients.
  • Public justice governs the fair distribution of resources, while care governs personal attention at the bedside.

Origins and Traditions of Care

Care ethics emerged from feminist philosophy and connects to diverse global traditions.

  • Carol Gilligan (1982) and Nel Noddings (1984) named care ethics, validating relational moral reasoning.
  • Feminist theory challenged the view that moral maturity consists solely of abstract, detached rules.
  • Care appears across cultures: Confucian ren, Buddhist karuṇā, ubuntu, and nursing traditions.
  • Virginia Held views care as a distinct relational ethic; this lesson treats care as an essential clinical virtue.

Complementary Frameworks

Care ethics identifies two longstanding weaknesses in principle-based theory, while principles provide essential safeguards for care.

Principles can encourage excessive detachment and formal impartiality, underestimating the legitimate role of emotion and relationship in clinical life.

Care re-centers the patient's individual experience within the medical system.

Principles provide public accountability, preventing personal bias, favoritism, and well-intentioned paternalistic overreach.

Principles reminded Meg that her affectionate intentions could not override Aunt March's clear choices.

Non-Abandonment in Practice

Overriding her refusal, because genuine care always requires clinical hospitalization. Documenting the refusal on the electronic chart and discontinuing all medical contact. Continuing to pressure her with persuasive arguments until she changes her mind. Continued clinical presence, home-based symptom support, and collaborative planning.

Synthesizing the Framework

Clinical ethics holds that principles and virtues are complementary. Care ethics charges principle-based theory with overvaluing impartiality and neglecting emotion. Partiality toward your own patient is legitimate; justice governs how care is distributed.
