Meet Dr. Trackwell

"Most people think ethics is about being a good person," says Dr. Philippa 'Pip' Trackwell, emergency physician and railway safety consultant. "In crisis management, ethics is about what to do when every available option breaks your heart."

  • A moral dilemma isn't just a tough choice between what's easy and what's right.
  • It is a conflict where fundamental moral principles collide, and choosing one means violating another.
  • To see the hidden rules governing our moral minds, philosophers build thought experiments — mental wind tunnels designed to isolate one variable at a time.

Case 1: The Spur (The Switch)

A runaway trolley barrels down the track toward five track workers who cannot hear it coming. You stand beside a switch lever.

  • If you do nothing, the trolley continues straight and kills the five workers.
  • If you pull the switch, the trolley turns onto a side track (the spur), where it will kill one worker.
  • In global psychological surveys, roughly 85% to 90% of people say it is morally permissible (or even obligatory) to pull the switch.
  • The math looks simple: 5 lives saved vs. 1 life lost = net +4 lives.
 Judith Jarvis Thomson · 1976

Case 2: The Footbridge

Same runaway trolley, same five workers on the track. This time there is no side spur. You stand on an arched footbridge above the rails.

  • Next to you leans a heavy stranger. If you push him over the railing, his body will jam the trolley's wheels and stop it before it reaches the five.
  • The man will die, but the five workers will survive. Your own body is too light to stop the train.
  • When presented with this case, the numbers flip: over 85% of people say it is completely wrong to push the man.
  • Yet the arithmetic is identical: 1 life sacrificed to save 5 = net +4 lives.

The Paradox of the Two Cases

Pulling a switch is an indirect mechanical motion, whereas pushing someone off a bridge is purely accidental On the footbridge, you use the stranger as a physical buffer, whereas on the spur death is a side effect Pushing a person off a bridge saves fewer net lives than pulling a switch to divert the speeding train Judith Jarvis Thomson · 1985

Case 3: The Loop

To test whether 'pushing' vs. 'pulling a lever' was the real issue, Judith Jarvis Thomson invented The Loop.

  • The side track isn't a dead end—it loops back around to hit the five workers from behind!
  • However, there is a heavy worker on the loop. If you divert the train, it hits him, stops, and the five survive.
  • If the track were empty, flipping the switch would do nothing to save the five.
  • Here you pull a lever (no physical touching), but you depend on the body to halt the train. Our intuitions suddenly wobble.

The Three Moral Levers

When human beings face moral dilemmas, our minds aren't running a single calculator. We weigh at least three distinct intuitive principles:

  • 1. Consequences (Outcomes): Maximizing net well-being and saving the greatest number of lives ($5 > 1$).
  • 2. Deontic Constraints (Rights & Respect): Respecting individual inviolability; forbidding using someone merely as a tool without consent.
  • 3. Agency & Causal Role: The moral difference between redirecting an existing danger vs. initiating new lethal violence.

Doing vs. Allowing & Double Effect

Two foundational philosophical concepts explain why the cases feel so different:

  • Doing vs. Allowing: We feel a stronger moral duty not to cause harm directly (negative duty) than we feel a duty to prevent harm from happening (positive duty).
  • The Doctrine of Double Effect: It can be morally permissible to perform an action with a foreseen bad side-effect (diverting a train that happens to kill someone), but impermissible to intend that bad effect as the means to your good end (using their body to stop the train).

Case 4: The Organ Transplant

Dr. Pip Trackwell moves from the rail yard to the intensive care unit. Five patients are dying of organ failure (heart, lungs, liver, two kidneys).

  • A healthy young traveler walks into the clinic for a routine checkup.
  • His tissue is a perfect match for all five dying patients.
  • If the surgeon quietly doses him with an overdose and harvests his organs, five patients walk out alive.
  • The math is identical: 1 life taken to save 5 = net +4 lives. Yet virtually 100% of people reject this as horrific murder.

Why the Hospital Breaks the Math

Why can't a hospital simply run the numbers like a railway switch?

  • Fiduciary Duty & Trust: A physician has a strict duty of non-maleficence ("first, do no harm") to each individual patient under their care.
  • Systemic Collapse: If doctors were permitted to sacrifice healthy visitors for the greater good, no one would ever enter a hospital again.
  • Inviolable Bodily Rights: Every individual possesses a moral right not to have their body seized as a spare-parts reservoir for the collective good.

Analyzing the Transplant Case

The surgeon initiates a brand new lethal harm against someone who was not in danger The surgeon violates the fundamental clinical duty of non-maleficence owed to their patient The healthy patient is used merely as a biological means to save other patients The transplant case results in fewer total surviving patients than the railway switch

When Scarcity Forces the Choice

Unlike the fictitious transplant surgeon, real emergency physicians face genuine tragic dilemmas during mass casualties, pandemics, and war.

  • Under Crisis Standards of Care, the demand for life-saving resources (ventilators, ICU beds, dialysis) vastly exceeds supply.
  • Doctors are not choosing between murder and duty; they are allocating scarce positive aid to patients who all face lethal illness.
  • When you cannot save everyone, how do you decide who receives treatment?

The Four Triage Tags (START Protocol)

In disaster medicine, responders assign standardized color-coded triage tags in seconds:

  • 🔴 Immediate (Red): Life-threatening injuries requiring immediate intervention to survive.
  • 🟡 Delayed (Yellow): Serious injuries requiring care, but stable enough to wait hours.
  • 🟢 Minor (Green): "Walking wounded" with minor injuries; can wait until urgent cases are stabilized.
  • ⚫ Expectant / Palliative (Black): Injuries so catastrophic that survival is unlikely even with maximal resources. Prioritized for comfort care and pain relief so scarce equipment can save others.

Three Ways to Allocate Scarce Beds

When two patients in the 'Red' category arrive for the last ICU bed, which moral rule should decide?

  • 1. Maximizing Life-Years (Utilitarian Efficiency): Give the bed to the patient with the highest probability of long-term survival and the most quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) remaining.
  • 2. Egalitarian Lottery (Procedural Equality): Give every competing candidate an equal, random mathematical chance through a lottery.
  • 3. First-Come, First-Served (Temporal Queue): Allocate to whoever arrived at the hospital doors first. (Prone to rewarding people with easy transportation or private cars).

From Triage to Algorithms

Trolley problems and triage protocols are no longer just textbook exercises—they are being hardcoded into machines.

  • Autonomous Vehicles: An autonomous car's brakes fail. Should it swerve into a concrete barrier (killing its passenger) to avoid hitting five pedestrians?
  • Automated Triage Algorithms: Hospital AI systems rank patients on organ transplant waiting lists and ICU admission queues using multi-variable clinical scorecards.
  • The Core Lesson: If we do not make our ethical commitments conscious, explicit, and accountable, machines will make them for us in the dark.

What Thought Experiments Actually Do

"A trolley problem isn't about trains," Dr. Trackwell smiles. "And a triage protocol isn't about arithmetic."

  • Thought experiments are diagnostic stress-tests for human moral intuitions.
  • They demonstrate that we care deeply about both the consequences of our choices and the moral constraints that protect individual rights.
  • Doing philosophy means learning to state your reasons clearly when these two valid moral demands pull in opposite directions.

End of the Line

Dr. Trackwell hangs up her signal lantern. As you begin your semester in ethics, keep these core principles in your diagnostic kit:

  • Consequentialism: Pay attention to outcomes and net lives saved ($5 > 1$).
  • Deontology: Respect inviolable rights; never treat people merely as tools or bodily buffers.
  • Doctrine of Double Effect: Distinguish between intended means and foreseen, unavoidable side-effects.
  • Crisis Triage: In tragic scarcity, transparent and fair allocation protocols prevent arbitrary harm.
