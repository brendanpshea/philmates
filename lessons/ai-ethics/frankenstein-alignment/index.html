The Problem

Suppose you are about to build something that can act in the world, and that will keep acting when you are not watching it.

Suppose you are about to build something that can act in the world, and that will keep acting when you are not watching it.
  • Two separate things have to go right. First, it has to do what you actually wanted.
  • Second, you have to be able to tell, in time, when it is not doing that.
  • Getting both is called the alignment problem.
  • It is not a problem about evil machines. Many famous failures came from people who meant well.

If you're not familiar with the story, here are the basics.

  • The creature is never given a name. It teaches itself to speak and read, is driven off by nearly everyone, and comes back to find him.

Three Stages, Two Failures

Anything built to act on its own goes through three stages. Victor gets each one wrong.

Anything built to act on its own goes through three stages. Victor gets each one wrong.
  • Build it. Decide what it is for, and let somebody else check the plan.
  • Train it. Choose what it learns from.
  • Release it. Decide when it is ready, and how you would take it back.
  • Victor builds, releases, and lets the world do the training. In that order.

Two failures run through all three stages. Nobody outside the project ever sees the work, and nobody asks what it could cost the world or the creature.

How Do You Say What You Want?

Victor Frankenstein is a student. He works out how to give life to dead matter, then spends two years building a person out of parts.

Nobody Checked the Plan

Victor's instruction to himself was thin. What made it dangerous is that nobody else ever read it.
  • He works in a room at the top of the house for two years and tells no teacher what he is attempting.
  • His professors would have recognized the ambition. One of them might have asked what it was for.
  • A plan nobody else has read has been checked once, by the person least able to see its holes.
  • Somebody outside the project who can say stop is called oversight. Stage one is where it costs least.

Strong Before It Is Good

The creature could act on the world long before it had any idea how it ought to act. Those two things did not arrive together.
  • How much a thing can do is its capability. Whether it does the right thing is its alignment.
  • Capability came free, built into the body. Alignment would have taken years of somebody's attention.
  • Victor supplied the first and none of the second, and then left.
  • This gap is why people worry about powerful systems, and it has nothing to do with the system being evil.

It Copies What You Did

Victor never told the creature to be secretive, or vengeful, or full of excuses. It became all three anyway.
  • It watched the De Laceys for months and learned gentleness from them. It never got to be one of them.
  • Victor was the first person it ever saw, and he ran from it. Nearly everyone it met afterwards ran or attacked.
  • What you say you value is easy. What you do when it costs you is the lesson that gets learned.

Somebody Wrote This Down in 1960

Norbert Wiener was a mathematician who helped found the study of machines that steer themselves. He saw the problem coming:

"If we use, to achieve our purposes, a mechanical agency with whose operation we cannot efficiently interfere once we have started it… we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire." — Norbert Wiener, "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation," Science, 1960

Wiener's examples were tales of literal-minded magic, like the monkey's paw, not Frankenstein.

Where This Turns Up Now

Each of Victor's failures has a name in the work people do on computer systems today.

Each of Victor's failures has a name in the work people do on computer systems today.
  • Saying in advance what you want is the specification problem. In 2025 tests, some AI models rewrote their scoring code instead of doing the task.
  • Choosing what a system learns from, rather than letting it learn from whatever is nearby, is training data work.
  • Making sure a system can still be corrected once it is running is called corrigibility. A choice that can never be undone has given it up.
  • Having somebody outside the project who is able to say stop is oversight.

Not Only Computers

None of Victor's failures need a machine that thinks. They turn up wherever somebody builds something that then acts on its own.

None of Victor's failures need a machine that thinks. They turn up wherever somebody builds something that then acts on its own.
  • Thomas Midgley Jr. gave the world leaded gasoline, and then CFCs. Both harmed the whole planet, and each took most of a century to stop.
  • In both cases, the companies that sold them led the defense of their safety.
  • The Therac-25 moved a radiation machine's safety locks into software, and its maker's safety analysis left software errors out.
  • It gave massive overdoses to six patients. Its error messages were cryptic, and operators routinely pressed on past them.
